Every test you'll learn in this subject — every single one — is some version of that question. The one-sample t-test is the simplest, cleanest version. Kaya dito tayo magsisimula.

---

Astronomers constantly compare a measurement with a number that already exists: a catalogue value, an instrument specification, a site-survey figure, a theoretical prediction, last season's calibration. The one-sample t-test is the formal way to ask whether the gap between our sample's mean and that number is larger than chance alone would produce.

After completing this module, you should be able to:

  3. Compute and explain the t statistic, degrees of freedom, p value, confidence interval, and Cohen's d.

Purpose: what the test does and when to use it

🎙 Lecture scriptWhat the test is for0:03 – 0:08

So what is a one-sample t-test? Simple lang. You have one sample — one group of measurements — and you want to compare its average with one fixed number that already exists. That number, we call the test value. The symbol is μ₀. Read it as "mu-zero." Mu is the Greek letter we use for the population mean, okay?

Now listen to the word fixed. Ang μ₀, hindi galing sa data mo. Galing sa labas. A catalogue value. An instrument spec from the manufacturer. A number in an earlier survey report. A theoretical prediction. Something that was already written down before you even started observing.

Point to the six cards.

Tingnan niyo — ang dami ng ganitong problema sa astronomy. Site testing: "Is the sky still 20.9 like the old report says?" Instrumentation: "Does our new camera really reach the limiting magnitude the manufacturer promised?" Variable stars: "Are the students' period estimates different from the catalogue period?" Observatory operations: "Are we really getting the 40 usable hours a month that the schedule assumes?" Photometry: "Is the sky background above the limit our pipeline can handle?" Spectroscopy: "Is the calibration line where the lab says it should be?" Same shape, all of them. One sample, one number. One test.

Now here's a rule, and I'm serious about this one. Point to the red box. You fix μ₀ before you look at your data. Hindi pwedeng makita mo muna na 21.08 ang average, tapos sasabihin mo, "Ay, i-compare natin sa 21.0." Bakit? Kasi pinili mo na ang kalaban ng data mo after mo makita 'yung data. Whatever answer comes out of that, wala nang kwenta. Fix the number first. Write down where it came from — which catalogue, which manual, which report. Then look.

Scroll to the comparison table.

One more thing, kasi every year may nagkakamali dito. The one-sample t-test is not for comparing two telescopes with each other — that's next chapter. It's not for measuring the same star before and after you change a filter — that's the paired test. Isa lang. One sample, one variable, one fixed number. If you can say the sentence "is our average different from this number," you're in the right place.

---

A one-sample t-test compares the mean of one sample with a known, hypothesized, normative, or benchmark value, called the test value (μ0). It is used when the population standard deviation (σ) is unknown and must be estimated from the sample — which, in observational work, is almost always.

💡Plain-language analogy

Think of a camera shutter commanded to open for 250 ms. No two exposures are identical, so a single frame timed at 247 ms proves nothing. But if the average of 30 frames is 247 ms and frames usually vary by only a millisecond or two, the shutter is probably mis-calibrated. The t-test formalises that judgement: it weighs the size of the gap against the natural wobble of averages.

⚠️The benchmark must come from outside the sample

μ0 is legitimate only if it was fixed before looking at the data: a catalogue, an instrument manual, a previous survey, a theoretical prediction, a proposal. Choosing the test value after inspecting the sample mean ("let us test against 21.0 because we got 21.08") destroys the meaning of the p value.

Part B

The statistical logic: effect divided by uncertainty

🎙 Lecture scriptThe logic: gap divided by wobble0:08 – 0:15

📍 On the page: Part B · The statistical logic (Lab 1 · t-distribution explorer) — scroll target #logic · Sidebar: B The statistical logic

Scroll to Part B. Show the formula box.

Okay, here's the whole test in one line. Ready?

t equals the sample mean minus μ₀, divided by the standard error.

Huwag kayong matakot sa fraction. Let's break it.

The top part is the gap. How far is your sample average from the old value? 21.08 minus 20.9. That's 0.18 magnitudes. Small number, but remember, magnitudes are a log scale — 0.18 mag is about an 18 percent difference in sky brightness. Hindi 'yan maliit para sa isang faint galaxy.

The bottom part is the wobble. How much does the average jump around if you observe another thirty nights, and another, and another? That's the standard error.

So t is just: gap divided by wobble. How many "wobbles" big is the gap? That's it. That's the entire test. Everything else is details.

Now I need you to separate two things na magkamukha pero magkaiba. Listen carefully, lumalabas 'to sa quiz.

Show the parameters table.

The standard deviation — s — is how much individual nights differ from each other. May 20.4, may 21.9. For Sitio Malaya, s is 0.37 mag. That's a fact about nights.

The standard error is how much the average would move from one set of thirty nights to another. Formula: s divided by the square root of n. 0.37 divided by the square root of 30 — root 30 is about 5.5 — so 0.067 mag. That's a fact about averages, not about nights.

Notice the square root. That little symbol is the most expensive symbol in this whole subject. Kung gusto mong maging kalahati ang wobble, hindi mo dinodoble ang sample. You need four times as many nights. Sa astronomy, nights are expensive. Every clear moonless night you spend on site testing is a night you didn't spend on your thesis. Precision costs, and it costs faster than people expect.

So for our site: gap is 0.18, wobble is 0.067. 0.18 divided by 0.067 is about 2.7. The average sits 2.7 wobbles above the old value. Now — is 2.7 wobbles a lot? That's what the t curve tells us.

Bakit t and not the normal curve? Kwento lang. In 1908 there was a man working at the Guinness brewery in Ireland named William Gosset. His job was testing barley, and he only ever had tiny samples. And he noticed: because we estimate the standard deviation from the sample instead of knowing the true one, the bottom of that fraction is shaky. Smaller sample, shakier. So the curve needs fatter tails to be honest about it. He published it under the pen name "Student" — kasi bawal mag-publish ang employees — and that's why we still call it Student's t.

How fat the tails are depends on the degrees of freedom, which is just n minus one. Bakit minus one? Ganito: if I tell you the average of thirty readings, twenty-nine of them can be anything — but the last one is stuck. It has to be whatever makes the average come out right. One degree of freedom was used up by the mean.

Open Lab 1. Set t = 2.10 and drag df to 4.

Tingnan niyo 'to. Same t, 2.10. Four degrees of freedom — like five nights only — the red shaded area, 'yan ang p value, is about .10. Not significant. Drag df to 60. Same t. Sixty degrees of freedom. Now p is about .04. Significant na. Walang nagbago sa evidence. Ang nagbago lang, how much we trust the standard deviation. Five nights, mas demanding ang curve. Sixty-one nights, relax na siya, halos normal curve na.

Point to the "What a p value is" box.

And now, once, clearly, para hindi na tayo mag-away for the rest of the semester. The p value is the probability of getting a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null is true. It is not "the chance na tsamba lang." When we get p = .011, ang ibig sabihin: if the sky were really still 20.9, only about one set of thirty nights in a hundred would come out this far away. That's why we start doubting the 20.9.

Parameters, statistics, and the standard error

QuantityPopulation (parameter)Sample (statistic)Meaning
Meanμx̄Centre
Standard deviationσsSpread of individual observations
Standard error of the meanσ/√ns/√nSpread of sample means across hypothetical repeated samples

If the site-testing team observed a different 30 nights, the mean would change. The standard error measures how much. It shrinks with the square root of n: quadrupling the sample only halves the uncertainty.

t = x̄ − μ0s / √n df = n − 1 Numerator: the observed effect. Denominator: the standard error. The ratio counts how many standard errors separate the sample mean from the benchmark.

Why Student's t and not the normal curve?

Because we substitute s for the unknown σ, the denominator is itself an estimate and adds uncertainty. William Gosset ("Student", 1908), a brewer at Guinness working with tiny samples of barley, showed that the ratio follows a distribution with heavier tails than the normal. The heaviness depends on the degrees of freedom. With few df, stronger evidence is required to reject H0; as df grows, t converges to N(0, 1).

Why n − 1? Once the mean is fixed, only n − 1 deviations are free to vary — the last is determined because deviations must sum to zero.

🧭What a p value is — and is not

The p value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis and the statistical model are true, of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that H0 is true, not the probability the result is "due to chance", and not a measure of effect size. Setting α = .05 adopts a decision rule that rejects a true null 5% of the time in the long run.

Step 1 of 7

Define the research question and hypotheses

🎙 Lecture scriptStep 1: the question and the hypotheses0:18 – 0:23

📍 On the page: Step 1 · Define the research question and hypotheses (Lab 2 · Hypothesis builder) — scroll target #step1 · Sidebar: 1 Research question & hypotheses

Scroll to Step 1.

Every good analysis starts on paper. Not in Excel, not in Python. Paper.

The hypotheses are statements about the population mean — the true average sky brightness over Sitio Malaya on all clear moonless nights, most of which nobody will ever measure. Hindi tungkol sa sample mean. You already know the sample mean, it's 21.08. Wala nang ite-test doon.

The null hypothesis says nothing changed: μ equals 20.9. The alternative says something changed: μ is not equal to 20.9.

Show the two-tailed / one-tailed table.

Someone might ask — sir, bakit "not equal"? Hindi ba ang takot natin ay lumiwanag ang langit dahil sa highway? Bakit hindi "less than"? Good question. Yes, the fear is a brighter sky. But what if the true sky is darker than the old report said? That matters too! Iba ang telescope na i-propose mo for a 21.3 sky versus a 20.9 sky. When a difference in either direction would change your decision, you go two-tailed. Two-tailed is your safe default. One-tailed is only allowed if you can justify the direction before you see the numbers — and you write that reason down.

Point to the "tail-switching" mistake.

Here's what people actually do, and huwag niyong gagayahin. Someone runs a two-tailed test, gets p = .08. Sayang, not significant. So they rerun it as one-tailed, "kasi expected naman namin na mas maliwanag," and now p = .04. Yehey, significant! No. Hindi. They chose the direction after seeing the data. Their real false-alarm rate is now ten percent while they're claiming five. Any referee who knows statistics will catch that in two seconds.

Open Lab 2. Type through it.

The hypothesis builder here writes it out for you. Variable: zenith night-sky brightness. Unit: mag per square arcsecond. Population: clear moonless nights at the Sitio Malaya site. Benchmark: 20.9, from the 2019 site-survey report. Direction: different. Alpha: .05.

That's the output of Step 1. A question, a benchmark with a source, two hypotheses, a direction, and an alpha — all fixed before we touch the data.

⚠️Common mistakes in Step 1
  • Hypotheses about the sample — writing H0: x̄ = 20.9. The sample mean is known; inference concerns μ.

Class, the test is only as good as the numbers under it. Garbage in, garbage out. And nobody's data is perfect — sa astronomy lalo na, may ulap, may satellite trail, may cosmic ray. What matters is that you looked, you wrote down what you found, and you explained what you did about it.

Thirty nights. Mean 21.08. Standard deviation 0.37. Darkest night, 21.88. Brightest, 20.38. Median 21.07 — very close to the mean. That's our first clue that the data are roughly balanced, hindi skewed.

Point to the boxplot.

Look at the picture. The gold line is the old value, 20.9. The blue box is the middle half of the nights. See how most of the box is to the right — the darker side? Each dot is one night. No red dots — nothing flagged as unusual.

Now let me show you what unusual looks like. Click the Eclipsing binary period pill. Different data. Twenty-two of your seniors each made a light curve of an eclipsing binary and estimated its period. The catalogue says 3.00 days. See that red dot way out at 8.8? One student got 8.8 days. Everyone else is between 2.8 and 4.5.

So what do you do with the 8.8?

Wait. Someone will say "delete it."

Sabi ko na. Somebody always says delete. No. Not yet. You ask four questions first. Is it a typo — did someone type 8.8 instead of 3.8? Is it a method error — 8.8 is almost three times 3.0, so maybe the student counted three cycles as one? That's a classic mistake in period finding. Is it real — no, the star doesn't change its orbit for one student. And does your question need it? If you're evaluating how well the class learned the method, that 8.8 is a very interesting data point about the student, but not about the star.

You never delete a data point just because it makes your p value look bad. You trace it — go back to that light curve. You decide. You write down why. And if you're not sure, run the analysis both ways and report both. That's called a sensitivity analysis, and honestly, that's the difference between someone who plays with data and someone you can trust with data.

The test is only as credible as the data beneath it. Good research is judged not on whether the data were perfect but on whether the researcher looked, documented, and justified. Keep the raw file untouched; perform all cleaning on a copy, and log each exclusion with a reason.

Step-by-step: the ten actions of Step 2
💡Outliers: investigate, never auto-delete

Before removing any case, ask four questions. Is it a data-entry error (8.8 typed for 3.8)? A measurement error (a cycle-count mistake, a cosmic-ray hit, a satellite trail)? A legitimate extreme (a night of unusual airglow)? Does the research question require keeping it (bright nights are exactly what the site survey must characterise)? Outliers must never be removed merely to obtain p < .05. When in doubt, run the analysis with and without the case and report both — a sensitivity analysis.

Step 3 of 7

Test for normality

🎙 Lecture scriptStep 3: normality — a judgement, not a light switch0:28 – 0:34

📍 On the page: Step 3 · Test for normality (Lab 4 · Normality diagnostics) — scroll target #step3 · Sidebar: 3 Test for normality

Scroll to Step 3.

The t-test assumes the population is normal — 'yung bell curve. Strictly speaking, that's what makes the math exact. But in practice, what really matters is that the average behaves normally, and the Central Limit Theorem — remember that from Chapter 4? — gives you that for free once your sample is decent in size. That's why people say the t-test is robust. Matibay siya.

Matibay, pero hindi indestructible. It's not robust against a small sample with one crazy outlier or a badly lopsided shape. So we check.

Open Lab 4.

Three kinds of evidence, and you use all three together, not just one.

The histogram. Is it roughly a hill? One peak, no long tail dragging to one side? Sitio Malaya looks like a hill. Good.

The Q–Q plot. This is honestly the best single picture you have. The dots are your data. The line is where the dots would sit if the data were perfectly normal. Dots hugging the line, normal. Dots making an S shape, heavy tails. Dots curving like a banana, skewed. Our nights hug the line.

The Shapiro–Wilk test. Here the null hypothesis is "the data are consistent with normal." W is .984, p is .915. No evidence against normality.

Point to the interpretation guide table.

Now, 'yung sinabi ko kanina tungkol sa diamond. Shapiro–Wilk is a test, and tests have power, and power depends on sample size. With eight nights, the test is almost blind — so p = .40 doesn't prove your data are normal, it just means the test couldn't see anything. With four hundred nights, the test has eagle eyes — it flags tiny wobbles that don't matter at all, so p = .03 on a nice symmetric histogram is not a reason to throw away the t-test. The decision comes from the plots, the test, and the sample size together. Kung may magsabi sa inyo "p less than .05, kaya Wilcoxon agad" — that person memorized a rule. Hindi niya naintindihan.

Click the Eclipsing binary pill, look at Lab 4.

For contrast — the period estimates. Skewness 3.8. W is .52, p less than .001. Look at the Q–Q plot, may isang dot na lumipad sa taas. This is what a real violation looks like. Twenty-two cases, one extreme value, and the Central Limit Theorem is not coming to save you. Remember this one — babalikan natin siya sa dulo.

  • Combine visual, statistical, and sample-size evidence into a defensible decision.

Strictly, the t-test assumes the population is normal, which guarantees that the t ratio follows Student's distribution exactly. In practice, what matters is that the sampling distribution of the mean is close to normal. The Central Limit Theorem delivers this for moderate and large samples, which is why the t-test is described as robust to mild non-normality. It is not robust to severe skew or extreme outliers in small samples.

✅Assumptions of the one-sample t-test
  1. Quantitative outcome. Interval or ratio measurement, so that a mean is meaningful. Multi-item composite scales are commonly treated as approximately continuous; a single Likert item is ordinal.
  2. One sample. A single group compared with a fixed value.
  3. Independent observations. Each case measured once; one case's value does not influence another's. This is the assumption that matters most and the one software cannot check.
  4. Approximate normality / no extreme outliers. Addressed in Step 3; especially important when n is small.

A fifth, often unstated requirement is representative sampling. The p value describes random sampling error only. It says nothing about bias from convenience samples, non-response, or cherry-picked time windows.

💡Independence: three ways it quietly fails

Clustering — thirty SQM readings taken minutes apart on the same night share that night's airglow, haze, and moon. Time dependence — fourteen consecutive nights of sky background are autocorrelated; tonight resembles last night, and the lunar phase drifts slowly. Repeated cases — the same star measured on three frames of the same field. In each case the data contain less information than n suggests, the standard error is understated, and p values look better than they should.

Read the worked solution line by line. Slowly.

Given: n = 30, mean 21.082, s = 0.368, μ₀ = 20.9.

Step one, standard error. 0.368 divided by the square root of 30. Root 30 is 5.477. So 0.067.

Step two, the test statistic. 21.082 minus 20.9 is 0.182. Divide by 0.067. t = 2.712.

Step three, degrees of freedom. 30 minus 1 is 29.

Step four, the p value. Two-tailed, so it's twice the area beyond 2.712 on a t curve with 29 degrees of freedom. That's .011. The cutoff — the critical value — at alpha .05 is plus or minus 2.045. Our 2.712 is past it.

Step five, the 95% confidence interval. 21.082 plus or minus 2.045 times 0.067. That gives 20.94 to 21.22.

Step six, the effect size. 0.182 divided by 0.368. Cohen's d is 0.50.

Point to the chart.

There's your t on its curve. The little red shaded parts are the p value — tiny. The gold dashed lines are the cutoffs. Your t is outside them.

Dito namamatay ang karamihan ng reports. They write "the result was significant, p < .05" — period, tapos na. That is the least useful sentence you can write about a result. Parang sinabi mo lang "may nangyari," pero hindi mo sinabi kung ano. A real interpretation answers five questions, in order.


One — is there evidence of a difference? p = .011, below .05. Reject the null. Yes, there's evidence.


Two — which direction? The sample mean is 0.18 mag above the old value. Higher number, darker sky. So the sky is darker than the 2019 report said — hindi pala mas maliwanag. Surprise. Maybe the 2019 team measured during a hazy season. Maybe the highway lights are shielded. We don't know yet — and notice, the test doesn't tell us.


Three — how big? In real units: 0.18 magnitudes, which is about 18 percent less sky brightness. In standard units: Cohen's d is 0.50. By Cohen's labels, that's "medium." But listen — those labels, .2 small, .5 medium, .8 large, were made up in 1988 for psychology experiments, as a last resort when nothing better was available. Whether 0.18 mag matters is an astronomy question, not a statistics question. If you're planning to image faint galaxies, 0.18 mag of extra darkness is a real gift. If you're doing bright-star photometry, wala lang. The statistic can't tell you which. You have to know the science.


Four — how sure are we? Open Lab 8, point to the CI plot. The interval runs from 20.94 to 21.22. Read it like this: every value in that range is a possible true sky brightness that the data would not reject. And 20.9 is outside it. That's exactly why the test was significant — the interval and the two-tailed test always agree. The interval just tells you more. It says the real sky could be barely darker than the old value, or a lot darker. That's the honest range.


And please, the wording. Not "there is a 95% probability that μ is inside this interval." Say "we are 95% confident." Meaning: if we did this a hundred times with a hundred sets of nights, about ninety-five of those intervals would catch the true mean.


Five — does it matter? That's the department's call, and the call of whoever designs the telescope. Our job is to give them the size, the direction, and the uncertainty, honestly, without exaggerating any of them.

Power. Two ways to be wrong. Type I error is a false alarm — you abandon a site that was actually fine. Type II error is a missed signal — you build a telescope on a site the new lights have already spoiled, because your thirty nights didn't catch it. Power is the chance of catching a real effect when it's really there.


Set d = 0.5, n = 30. For a medium effect, thirty nights gives you 75% power. Meaning one time in four, a real change of this size would come out not significant, and the team would say "okay lang pala." Thirty-four nights gets you to 80%. Drag d to 0.2. A small effect? You need about two hundred nights. Two hundred clear moonless nights — that's years. So decide your sample size before you go to the site, not after the result disappoints you.


Scroll to the large-sample paradox table.


And the opposite problem. Hold the effect at a tiny d of 0.10 — one-tenth of a standard deviation, basically nothing — and just grow the sample. n = 10, not significant. n = 400, significant. n = 5,000, p is microscopic. The effect never changed. Only the precision did. Sa era ng big surveys — Gaia, LSST, millions of stars — this is the trap. Kaya never, ever send a p value out alone. 💡Wilcoxon signed-rank in one paragraph
Subtract μ0 from each value, discard zeros, rank the absolute differences, then sum the ranks of the positive differences (W+). If the distribution is centred at μ0, positive and negative ranks should balance. Because ranks cap the influence of extreme values, one wild observation cannot dominate. It has assumptions of its own — notably a symmetric distribution of differences.

    
💡Bootstrap in one paragraph
Resample your n observations with replacement thousands of times, compute the mean each time, and take the middle 95% of those means as a confidence interval. No formula for the sampling distribution is required — the data approximate it. The Interpretation dashboard (Lab 8) runs 5,000 resamples with a fixed seed, so results are reproducible.

Instructor notes


  • If you are running short: Lab 7 (summary-statistics calculator) and the software tabs can be skipped and assigned as reading. Do not cut the binary-star contrast at 0:54 — that's the part students remember.
  • If you are running long: replace the large-sample paradox table with one sentence.
  • Figures quoted (Sitio Malaya: t(29) = 2.71, p = .011, 95% CI 20.94, 21.22, d = 0.50; binary period: t p = .054 vs Wilcoxon p = .004, t p = .007 without the 8.8; shutter: t(15) = 1.19, p = .252, CI 249.57, 251.52; power: n = 34 for d = 0.5 at 80%, about 199 for d = 0.2) are computed live by the page, so they match what students see on screen.
  • The magnitude scale. Say "bigger number, darker sky" at least three times during the hour; it is the single most common confusion in the site-testing discussion. The 0.18 mag ≈ 18 % remark uses the small-difference approximation (a factor of 10^(0.4×0.18) ≈ 1.18).
  • Likely questions. "Sir, bakit hindi na lang z?" — because σ is unknown; z is the textbook case. "Pwede bang tanggalin 'yung 8.8?" — only with a written reason (go back to the light curve), and report both analyses. "Is d = 0.5 good or bad?" — wrong question; ask what 0.18 mag does to your faintest target.
  • Language. The Tagalog phrases are there for warmth and rhythm; swap any of them for English or drop them if the class mix calls for it. The numbers and the argument do not change.

  

  

  
Key takeaways

  
    
    
  1. A one-sample t-test asks whether one mean differs from a benchmark fixed in advance.
    2. 
    
  2. t = effect ÷ uncertainty. A large t can come from a big effect, a small standard error, or both.
    3. 
    
  3. Independence is the assumption that matters most and the one software cannot check.
    4. 
    
  4. Normality is a judgement built from plots, tests, and sample size — not a single p value.
    5. 
    
  5. Report the mean difference, confidence interval, and effect size alongside p.
    6. 
    
  6. Statistical significance is not scientific significance; non-significance is not proof of no difference.
    7. 
    
  7. Plan the sample size for adequate power before collecting data.
    8. 
    
  8. The test shows that a mean departs from a benchmark, never why.
    9.
