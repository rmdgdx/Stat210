2.1Why description comes first

Chapter 1 gave you the licence to make a claim. This chapter is about earning the right to compute one — because before a single test is run, the data has to be trustworthy and you have to know its shape.

Here is the sequence almost every failed analysis follows. Someone exports a spreadsheet, drops it into a tool, and asks for the average. The tool obliges. The average is wrong — not because the arithmetic failed, but because one station reported in cubic metres while the others reported in thousands, or a missing value was stored as 0, or the same station appears twice. None of those problems announce themselves. They just quietly shift the number, and the number gets presented.

🧠 The two jobs of description

Job 1 — communicate. Summarise the data so a reader or decision-maker can grasp it: a centre, a spread, a shape, a chart.

Job 2 — diagnose. Find out what kind of data you are holding, so you know which inferential procedure is legal later. Is it skewed? Are there outliers? Are the groups equally variable? Every assumption you will check in Sessions 6 through 12 is checked here, with descriptive tools.

Urban consistently underrate Job 2. They treat the mean and the histogram as things you produce for the report's opening section and then move past. In research practice it is the reverse: the descriptive phase is where you decide whether a t-test is defensible at all. A histogram is not decoration. It is evidence about whether your later test is valid.

⚠️ Identical summaries, different data

Two datasets can share the same mean, the same standard deviation, and the same correlation, and still describe completely different worlds — one a clean linear trend, another a curve, another a flat line with a single wild point. This is the classic demonstration that summary statistics alone can never substitute for looking at the data. The professional habit: plot first, summarise second, test third. In that order, every time.

🏁 30-second recap

Description communicates and diagnoses. The diagnostic job is the one that protects your later inference. Plot → summarise → test.

2.2Data preparation: the unglamorous 80%

Practising analysts spend the majority of their time here, and almost none of their reporting on it. That asymmetry is exactly why data-preparation errors survive into monitoring reports.

Missing data — and the cardinal sin

Missing values arrive in three flavours, and the distinction matters because it determines whether ignoring them biases your result:

🎲Missing completely at randomThe gap has nothing to do with anything. A scanner glitched. Dropping these rows costs precision but not validity.
🔗Missing at randomThe gap depends on something you did observe — e.g. newer stations skip a field. Fixable if you model the known driver.
🚨Missing not at randomThe gap depends on the missing value itself. Stations with faulty gauges "forget" to report streamflow. This is the dangerous one — the absence is the signal.
🚫 The cardinal sin: coding missing as zero

A blank cell means we do not know. A zero means we know, and it is nothing. These are opposite statements, and storing the first as the second is the single most common data-preparation error in applied reporting.

In this chapter's case study, one station's unreported streamflow was stored as 0. That single cell pulled the group mean down by 41.78 thousand m³ — 7.7%, and inflated the standard deviation, all while looking like a legitimate observation. A blank would have been excluded automatically. A zero was silently averaged in.

Your three options, and what each costs

ApproachWhat it doesWhat it costs you
Listwise deletionDrop any record with a missing field.Shrinks n, and — unless the data is MCAR — quietly redefines your population. You are now describing "stations that report", not "stations".
Mean imputationSubstitute the variable's mean for the gap.Preserves n and the mean, but artificially shrinks the variance, because you added values with zero deviation. Your spread now understates reality.
Flag and reportExclude from the computation, but count and disclose the omissions.Nothing, except honesty. This is the professional default for coursework and for practice.

Outliers — three questions before you touch anything

An outlier is an observation far from the rest. It is not automatically an error, and deleting it because it is inconvenient is data fabrication. Ask, in order:

  1. Is it an error? A typo, a unit mix-up, a decimal in the wrong place, a sensor fault. → Correct it if you can establish the true value; otherwise treat it as missing.
  2. Is it a genuine extreme from the same population? A real main-stem gauge that really does carry more water than the rest. → Keep it, report it, and use statistics that are honest about it (median, IQR) alongside the mean.
  3. Is it from a different population altogether? A reservoir-release reading recorded in a natural-flow dataset. → Exclude it, and state the exclusion rule you applied — before you looked at the results, ideally.
💼 Why this is an ethics question, not a technique question

Deleting a point moves your headline number. That makes the deletion rule a decision with a real consequence for anyone who acts on the figure, which is precisely why it must be written down before you see the outcome. "We excluded observations beyond three standard deviations" is a method. "We excluded the station that was ruining our average" is misconduct wearing the same clothes.

The other four defects, all of which hide in plain sight

👯Duplicate recordsThe same station, sample or respondent counted twice. Inflates n, and drags the mean toward whatever was duplicated. Check for exact repeats on a key field before anything else.
📏Unit inconsistencyCubic metres mixed with thousands of cubic metres; grams with kilograms; percentages stored sometimes as 0.42 and sometimes as 42. Catastrophic and completely invisible in a column of numbers.
🎚️Scale mismatchOne respondent group answered on 1–5, another on 1–10. Averaging the pooled column produces a number that describes nothing. Rescaling is possible but is itself an assumption.
🔤Inconsistent coding"Female", "F", "f", "FEMALE" as four categories. Reverse-scored items left un-reversed. Dates as text. Trailing spaces. All silently split or merge groups.
📌 The codebook and the audit trail

A codebook records, for every variable: its exact name, what it measures, its unit, its level of measurement, its permitted range, how categories are coded, and how missing values are represented. It is what lets a second analyst reproduce your work — and what lets you reproduce it yourself in six months.

The audit trail is the companion log of every change made to the raw data, with the reason. Never edit the raw file. Keep the original untouched, script or log your corrections, and produce a cleaned copy. If you cannot state how many rows you dropped and why, your analysis is not reproducible, and in this course it is not gradable.

🧪 Try it — spot the defect

A column of monthly streamflows (thousand m³) reads: 512, 486, 455, 470, 0, 498, 505, 493000. Two separate defects are present. Name both and say what each does to the mean.

Answer: the 0 is almost certainly a missing value coded as zero — it drags the mean down. The 493000 is a unit error, cubic metres where the column is thousands — it drags the mean up, catastrophically. Note that the two errors push the total in opposite directions — though the offset is trivial, 490 against 492,507, so the unit error dominates completely while both corrupt the spread.

🏁 30-second recap

Missing ≠ zero. Investigate outliers before deleting them, and write the rule down first. Hunt duplicates, unit errors, scale mismatches and coding drift. Keep a codebook, keep an audit trail, never edit the raw file.

2.3Organising data: distributions and cross-tabs

Once the data is clean, the first act of description is to organise it — to turn a column of numbers into a shape you can see.

Building a frequency distribution

A frequency distribution groups a quantitative variable into classes and counts how many observations fall in each. Four decisions, in order:

  1. How many classes? Too few hides the shape; too many turns the histogram into noise. A common guide is Sturges' rule:
    k ≈ 1 + 3.322 · log10(n)
    For n = 12: k ≈ 1 + 3.322(1.079) = 4.59, so use 5 classes. Treat it as a starting point, not a law — always look at the resulting histogram and adjust.
  2. How wide? Divide the range by k and round up to a convenient number. Take the twelve-station dataset introduced in §2.4, which runs from 420 to 1,850 — a range of 1,430. Then 1,430 ÷ 5 = 286, so a width of 300 is clean and covers the data.
  3. Where do classes start? Choose a lower limit at or below the minimum, on a round number. Classes must be mutually exclusive (no value fits two) and exhaustive (every value fits one).
  4. Compute the companions. Class boundaries (the true dividing points, halfway between adjacent limits), the midpoint (used as the class's representative value), relative frequency (the proportion), and cumulative frequency (the running total).
⚠️ Follow the rule blindly and watch it fail

Apply those 5 classes of width 300, starting at 400, to that dataset and you get: 400–700 → 11 stations, 700–1,000 → 0, 1,000–1,300 → 0, 1,300–1,600 → 0, 1,600–1,900 → 1. Three empty classes and a histogram that shows almost nothing. That is not a failure of your arithmetic — it is Sturges' rule meeting a skewed distribution with an outlier, a case its assumptions do not cover. The lesson is the one stated above: the rule proposes, the histogram decides. Here the honest answer is to chart the eleven ordinary stations and report the main-stem gauge separately.

⚠️ Grouping always destroys information

Once you group, every observation in a class is treated as sitting at the midpoint. That is why a mean computed from grouped data differs slightly from the mean of the raw values — the grouped version is an approximation. Group to see the distribution; compute from the raw data whenever you still have it.

Cross-tabulation — and the percentage that answers your question

A cross-tabulation (contingency table) counts the joint frequencies of two categorical variables. It is the descriptive foundation of the chi-square tests you will meet in Session 9. The technique is trivial. The trap is not.

Consider 400 households classified by locality and by whether they adopted rainwater harvesting after an information campaign:

Percentage across the explanatory variable. If you think locality influences adoption, then locality defines the groups you are comparing, so each locality's row must sum to 100%. Urban households adopt at 60%, rural at 24% — that comparison is meaningful. Reporting instead that "60% of adopters were urban" invites the reader to confuse a profile with a rate, and it is one of the most common ways a table misleads without containing a single wrong number.

🏁 30-second recap

Sturges gives you a starting k; the histogram decides. Classes: mutually exclusive, exhaustive, round limits. Grouping approximates. In a cross-tab, percentage across the explanatory variable — the denominator is the question.

2.4Measures of centre

🌊 The dataset we will compute everything from

The Marikina network operates 12 stream-gauging stations. Monthly streamflow, in thousand m³, sorted ascending:

420   455   470   480   495   510   520   540   565   590   640   1,850

The last station is the Wawa main-stem gauge, just below the confluence of the two largest tributaries. It is a genuine extreme value, not an error — which is exactly what makes it instructive. Every statistic in sections 2.4 to 2.7 is computed from these twelve numbers, by hand, so you can see precisely what each one does when a real outlier is present.

A measure of centre answers "if I had to name one number that stands for this group, what would it be?" There are three candidates and they are not interchangeable.

The arithmetic mean

Mean — the balance point x̄ = Σx / n Σx = 420 + 455 + 470 + 480 + 495 + 510 + 520 + 540 + 565 + 590 + 640 + 1,850 = 7,535 x̄ = 7,535 / 12 = 627.92 thousand m³

The mean uses every value, which is its strength and its weakness. It is the balance point of the distribution — the value at which deviations above and below cancel exactly. That property makes it the foundation of variance, the t-test, ANOVA and regression, which is why it dominates statistics. It also means every observation can move it, including one absurd one.

The median

Median — the positional middle n = 12, which is even, so the median is the mean of the 6th and 7th ordered values. 6th = 510    7th = 520 Median = (510 + 520) / 2 = 515 thousand m³
⚠️ Look at the gap

Mean 627.92 thousand m³ versus median 515 thousand m³ — the mean is 21.9% higher, and it sits above ten of the twelve stations — only the 640 thousand m³ station and the main-stem gauge exceed it. If a basin hydrologist reported "our typical station carries about 628 thousand m³ a month", that statement would be approximately true of exactly one station, the 640 thousand m³ one, and would overstate the other ten by between 4% and 50%. The median is the honest answer to "typical" here. The mean is the honest answer to "total flow divided by station count" — a different question.

The mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value. In this dataset every value appears exactly once, so there is no mode. That is a legitimate outcome, not a failure — and it illustrates why the mode is of little use for continuous measurement data. Where the mode earns its keep is with nominal variables, where it is the only valid measure of centre: the most common land-cover class, the most frequently recorded species, the most common soil type.

The weighted mean — the one students get wrong on exams

When the values being averaged represent groups of unequal size, a plain average silently treats a 12,000-household stratum as equally important as a 1,000-household one. Use:

x̄w = Σ(w · x) / Σw
StratumResponse rate, xHouseholds, ww · x
Urban18%12,000216,000
Peri-urban34%3,000102,000
Rural52%1,00052,000
Total—16,000370,000
Simple vs weighted Simple average = (18 + 34 + 52) / 3 = 34.67% Weighted average = 370,000 / 16,000 = 23.13% The simple average overstates the true overall response rate by 11.54 percentage points.

Plan the follow-up survey on 34.67% and you will budget for responses that never arrive. The reason is simple: three quarters of the households sit in the stratum with the lowest response rate, and the simple average throws that fact away. Any time you average rates, ratios, or percentages across groups of different sizes, weight them.

Choosing between them

MeasureUse it whenAvoid it whenResistant to outliers?
MeanData is roughly symmetric; you need it for later inference; you want a total-preserving averageThe distribution is skewed or contains genuine extremesNo — every value moves it
MedianData is skewed; outliers are present; the variable is ordinal; you want "typical"You need the statistic to feed a t-test, ANOVA or regressionYes — highly
ModeThe variable is nominal; you want the most common categoryThe data is continuous and values rarely repeatYes
Weighted meanAveraging rates or ratios across groups of unequal sizeThe groups genuinely are equally weightedNo
🧠 The professional answer is usually "both"

Reporting the mean and the median together is not indecision — it is information. When they agree, the distribution is roughly symmetric and either will do. When they diverge, the gap itself is the finding: it tells the reader that a minority of extreme values is doing a lot of work. 628 thousand m³ mean against a 515 thousand m³ median says more about the Marikina network than either number alone.

🏁 30-second recap

Mean = balance point, uses everything, chases outliers. Median = positional middle, resistant, the honest "typical" for skewed data. Mode = nominal only. Weight your averages whenever group sizes differ. Report mean and median together and let the gap speak.

2.5Measures of variability

A centre without a spread is a half-truth. Two stations averaging 500 thousand m³ a month are completely different rivers if one ranges from 480 to 520 and the other from 150 to 900 — the first you can size a water-supply intake for; the second you cannot.

Range — fast, and almost useless

Range Range = maximum − minimum = 1,850 − 420 = 1,430 thousand m³

The range uses exactly two of the twelve numbers and ignores the rest, so a single extreme value defines it entirely. Here the range is 1,430 while the middle half of the stations spans only 102.5 — a factor of fourteen. Report the range as a quick sanity check on data entry; never rely on it to describe spread.

Variance and standard deviation

The idea: measure how far each observation sits from the mean, then average those distances. Raw deviations sum to exactly zero (that is what "balance point" means), so we square them first.

🔍 Read that squared-deviation column again

The eleven ordinary stations contribute 176,435 between them. The main-stem gauge alone contributes 1,493,488 — 89.4% of the entire sum of squares. Because deviations are squared, a value three standard deviations out carries roughly nine times the weight of one a single deviation out. This is why the standard deviation is even less resistant to outliers than the mean, and why an outlier check must precede any statement about spread.

Why divide by n − 1?

Because you already spent one piece of information estimating x̄. Once eleven of the twelve deviations are known, the twelfth is forced — deviations must sum to zero — so only 11 deviations are free to vary. That count is the degrees of freedom. Dividing by n instead of n − 1 produces a variance that is systematically too small: a biased estimator. Use n − 1 for a sample, N for a full population census. In this course, assume sample unless told otherwise.

The coefficient of variation — spread you can compare

The standard deviation carries the units of the data, so you cannot compare 389.63 thousand m³ against a standard deviation of 4.2 minutes. The coefficient of variation strips the units out by expressing spread as a percentage of the mean:

CV = (s / x̄) × 100%
CV for the Marikina network CV = (389.63 / 627.92) × 100% = 62.05%

A CV above 60% describes a network whose stations barely resemble one another. And here is the diagnostic payoff — recompute after setting the main-stem gauge aside:

Eleven of the Marikina network's stations are remarkably consistent — a CV of 12% is a set of tributaries that behave alike. The network-level figure of 62% does not describe a volatile basin; it describes one main-stem gauge and eleven ordinary tributary stations pooled into a single average that fits neither. The right recommendation is not "reduce variability". It is "report the main-stem gauge separately, because it is a different kind of river." Notice that no test was run to reach that conclusion. Description got you there.

🏁 30-second recap

Range is fragile. Variance squares deviations, so outliers dominate it — the main-stem gauge alone was 89% of the sum of squares. Divide by n − 1 because one deviation is not free. CV makes spread comparable across units and across variables. Empirical rule needs a bell shape; Chebyshev never does.

2.6Measures of position

Centre and spread describe the group. Position describes one observation relative to the group — which is what a field hydrologist actually asks: "is this station reading low, or is it normal?"

Percentiles and percentile rank

A percentile is a value below which a given percentage of observations fall. Two directions, two formulas.

Direction 1 — a value → its rank Percentile rank = (number of values below x + 0.5) / n × 100 For the 540 thousand m³ station: 7 stations fall below it. = (7 + 0.5) / 12 × 100 = 7.5 / 12 × 100 = 62.5 → the 63rd percentile
Direction 2 — a rank → its value c = n · p / 100, where p is the desired percentile For P90: c = 12(90) / 100 = 10.8 → not a whole number, so round up to 11. The 11th value in ascending order is 640 thousand m³ If c had come out whole, you would average the c-th and (c+1)-th values instead.

Quartiles and the interquartile range

Q1 and Q3 Q1: c = 12(25) / 100 = 3 → whole, so average the 3rd and 4th values = (470 + 480) / 2 = 475 Q3: c = 12(75) / 100 = 9 → whole, so average the 9th and 10th values = (565 + 590) / 2 = 577.5 IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 577.5 − 475 = 102.5 thousand m³
🧠 The IQR is the outlier-resistant standard deviation

The IQR spans the middle 50% of the data and is computed entirely from positions, so extreme values cannot touch it. Compare: the standard deviation is 389.63 thousand m³ and the IQR is 102.5 thousand m³ — a factor of nearly four, from the same twelve numbers. When a distribution is skewed, the correct pairing to report is median + IQR, exactly as mean + standard deviation is the correct pairing for symmetric data. Mixing them — a median with a standard deviation — signals that the analyst has not thought about it.

Detecting outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule

Fences Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5(IQR) = 475 − 1.5(102.5) = 475 − 153.75 = 321.25 Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5(IQR) = 577.5 + 153.75 = 731.25 1,850 thousand m³ lies far beyond the upper fence → flagged as an outlier. Nothing falls below 321.25, so there are no low outliers.

z-scores — position in standard-deviation units

z = (x − x̄) / s
The main-stem gauge z = (1,850 − 627.92) / 389.63 = 1,222.08 / 389.63 = +3.14 The lowest station: z = (420 − 627.92) / 389.63 = −0.53

A z-score is unit-free, which makes it the standard tool for comparing an observation's standing across different variables. A station at z = +1.2 on streamflow and z = −0.4 on water clarity is above average on one and slightly below on the other, and you can say that without the volume and index scales getting in the way. Conventionally |z| > 2 is unusual and |z| > 3 is a strong outlier candidate.

⚠️ The circularity trap in z-score outlier detection

The main-stem gauge's z-score of +3.14 is computed using a mean and a standard deviation that the main-stem gauge itself inflated. Because it dragged s up from 63.77 thousand m³ to 389.63 thousand m³, it made its own z-score smaller than it should be — against the eleven-station spread, 1,850 thousand m³ sits over 20 standard deviations out. This is why the IQR method is the more reliable detector: its fences are built from positions that outliers cannot influence. Use z-scores to describe position; use the IQR rule to find outliers.

The five-number summary and the boxplot

Five values describe a distribution's centre, spread and shape at once:

Minimum  ·  Q1  ·  Median  ·  Q3  ·  Maximum
420  ·  475  ·  515  ·  577.5  ·  1,850
🌊 What a hydrologist reads off this in five seconds
  • Half the network carries between 475 and 577.5 thousand m³ a month — a tight, predictable band.
  • The median station carries 515 thousand m³.
  • One station is in a different league and should be modelled, rated and benchmarked separately.
  • There is no dry tail — nothing falls below the lower fence, so no station is running unusually low.
🏁 30-second recap

Percentile rank = (below + 0.5)/n × 100. For a percentile's value, c = np/100 — round up if fractional, average two values if whole. IQR = Q3 − Q1, resistant. Fences at Q1 − 1.5·IQR and Q3 + 1.5·IQR find outliers; z-scores describe position. Pair median with IQR, mean with SD.

2.7Shape: skewness and kurtosis

Shape is the descriptive property with the most direct consequences for what you are allowed to do next. Nearly every parametric test in this course assumes approximate normality, and shape is how you check.

Skewness — which way the tail runs

Skewness measures asymmetry. The quickest practical detector needs only three numbers you already have:

Pearson's median skewness = 3(x̄ − median) / s
the Marikina network = 3(627.92 − 515) / 389.63 = 3(112.92) / 389.63 = 338.75 / 389.63 = +0.87
ShapeMean vs medianCoefficientTypical variables
Right-skewed (positive)mean > median> 0Rainfall totals, river discharge, pollutant concentrations, reaction times, hospital length of stay, household income, species abundance — most floored, unbounded-above quantities
Symmetricmean ≈ median≈ 0Measurement error, heights, well-controlled process times, exam scores
Left-skewed (negative)mean < median< 0Age at death in a developed population, scores near a ceiling, exam scores on an easy test

As a working guide: |skew| below 0.5 is approximately symmetric, 0.5 to 1 is moderate, above 1 is strong. The Marikina network's +0.87 is moderate-to-strong right skew.

📈 Right-skewed
The bulk of observations sit low, a thin tail stretches right, and the mean is pulled above the median. The mean no longer describes a typical case.
📊 Roughly symmetric
Mean and median all but coincide. Either is a fair summary, and the parametric toolkit is on much safer ground.

Figure 2.2 — the mean-median gap is the visual signature of skew. Grouped means and medians were computed from the class midpoints, so they are approximations of the underlying raw values.

⚠️ Why right skew is the default for floored quantities

Money and time variables have a hard floor and no ceiling. Streamflow cannot fall below zero, but there is nothing stopping one station from carrying ten times the average. Processing time cannot be negative, but a single sample can take a week. That asymmetry in the underlying process produces right skew almost every time — so if you compute a mean on a floored quantity such as rainfall, discharge or waiting time without checking skew, you should assume you have overstated the typical case until proven otherwise.

Kurtosis — and the definition almost everyone gets wrong

Kurtosis is routinely taught as "peakedness". That is misleading. Kurtosis is primarily about the tails — how much of the variance comes from rare extreme values rather than from typical ones. A distribution can look flat in the middle and still be heavy-tailed.

🗼Leptokurtic
Excess kurtosis > 0. Heavy tails — extreme events are more common than a normal curve predicts. Daily rainfall and river discharge are classic cases, which is why models assuming normality underestimate flood risk.
🔔Mesokurtic
Excess kurtosis ≈ 0. The normal benchmark. (Raw kurtosis for a normal distribution is 3, so software usually reports excess = raw − 3.)
🏔️Platykurtic
Excess kurtosis < 0. Thin tails — extremes are rarer than normal. Values cluster within a bounded range, as with a capped or rationed quantity.
🧠 Why shape is not a cosmetic detail

Shape is the bridge from this chapter to the rest of the course. Concretely:

  • Strong skew → the mean is a poor summary, and t-tests and ANOVA rest on shakier ground. Options: report the median, transform the variable, or move to the nonparametric counterparts in Session 12.
  • Heavy tails → extreme observations are more frequent than the normal model expects, so your intervals will be too narrow and your extreme-event estimates too optimistic.
  • Approximate symmetry with no extreme outliers → the full parametric toolkit is defensible, and you may say so explicitly in your methodology.
🏁 30-second recap

Skew = asymmetry; 3(x̄ − median)/s is the fast detector; floored quantities skew right by default. Kurtosis = tails, not peaks; heavy tails mean more extremes than normal predicts. Shape determines which tests are legal in Sessions 6–12.

2.8Choosing a chart

A chart is not an illustration of your analysis. It is an argument, and its axis, ordering and title are the premises. This section gives you a rule for picking the form and a framework for critiquing any chart you meet.

Start with the data's job, not the chart menu

The single most reliable way to pick a chart is to name what the data has to do for the reader. Everything follows from that.

The data's jobForm that does itWhy
Compare magnitude across categoriesBar chart — horizontal and sorted if labels are long or categories manyLength from a common zero baseline is the most accurately judged visual encoding humans have.
Show change over timeLine chartSlope carries the trend; connecting points asserts continuity, which is legitimate only for ordered time.
Show a distributionHistogram, boxplot, or dot plotYou need shape, centre, spread and outliers — the things a bar of the mean actively hides.
Show a relationshipScatterplotTwo positional encodings; the only form that reveals curvature and influential points.
Show parts of one wholeStacked bar, or a pie with 2–3 slices at mostAngle and area are judged poorly; only use them when precision genuinely does not matter.
Deliver a single headlineNot a chart — a large number with a labelOne value plotted as one bar is a number wearing a costume. Write the number.
✖ Wrong form
Nine slices. To rank Nangka against Tumana you must compare two angles that are neither adjacent nor aligned. There is no order, the legend forces a constant back-and-forth, and nine hues means several are near-indistinguishable.
✔ Right form
Same data, sorted horizontal bars. Ranking is instant, labels sit beside their bars so no legend is needed, one hue suffices because there is one series, and the title states the finding rather than the topic.

Figure 2.3 — the data did not change. Only the encoding did.

The action title — the highest-leverage edit in applied reporting

Most charts are titled with their topic. A title should carry the finding. This is the cheapest improvement available to you and the one most consistently ignored.

✖ Topic title
"Figure 4: Monthly Sensor Dropout Rate." Accurate, and it delegates all the work to the reader. Every viewer must derive the finding themselves — and some will derive a different one, or none.
✔ Action title
"Sensor dropout has doubled since the April firmware update." The title states the finding, the subtitle states the implication, an annotation marks the cause under investigation, and only the endpoint is labelled. Same numbers, a decision-ready chart.

Figure 2.4 — the action title makes a claim, which means it can be challenged. That is a feature: it moves the conversation to the evidence.

🧪 Try it — rewrite three titles

Turn each topic title into an action title. There is no single right answer; a good one names the finding and, ideally, the implication.

1. "Rainfall by Region, 2025"  →  e.g. "Visayas received 14% more rain than normal while Luzon was flat — move the Q1 drought watch north."
2. "Student Attrition by Programme"  →  e.g. "Attrition in Engineering is triple the university average."
3. "Water Clarity Scores by Station"  →  e.g. "Clarity fell at every station downstream of the quarry."

🏁 30-second recap

Name the data's job first; the job picks the form. Bars for magnitude, lines for time, histograms and boxplots for distribution, scatter for relationship, a written number for a headline. Critique along Story, Signs, Purpose, Perception, Method, Charts. Put the finding in the title.

2.9Colour, junk, and the axis crimes

Everything so far was about choosing the right form. This section is about the four ways a correctly-chosen chart still lies — and one of them we are going to catch in our own palette.

Colour discipline: colour has exactly four jobs

🏷️CategoricalIdentity. Distinct hues, assigned in a fixed order and never recycled.
🌡️SequentialMagnitude. One hue, light → dark. Never a rainbow.
↔️DivergingPolarity around a meaningful midpoint. Two hues with a neutral grey centre.
🚦StatusState: good / warning / serious / critical. Reserved — never reused as "series 4".

If a colour is not doing one of those four jobs, it is decoration, and decoration in a chart competes with the data for the reader's attention. The corollary most students miss: a single-series chart needs only one colour. Painting each bar a different hue implies the categories differ in kind when the only thing that differs is their value.

🔬 Our own palette fails as a data palette — and we measured it

This chapter is styled in seaborn's Pastel1. Those nine pale fills are excellent for what they are used for here: callout surfaces, section tags, table headers. They are unusable as series colours, and this is not an aesthetic opinion. Run the palette through a colour validator and it fails four separate checks:

🧠 And never rely on colour alone

Even a validated palette gets a second encoding. Every multi-series figure in this chapter carries a legend and direct labels, so a reader who cannot separate two hues can still read the chart. Colour should be the fastest way to identity, never the only way.

Axis crime 1 — the truncated baseline

A bar encodes quantity as length from zero. Move the baseline and the lengths no longer represent the numbers; they represent distance from an arbitrary line the designer chose.

✖ Truncated axis
The final bar looks about four times the first. The actual increase is 3%. The bars are drawing distance from 9.8 mg/L, and the headline then reads the picture instead of the data.
✔ Zero baseline
The same four numbers, honestly encoded. The visual is boring because the year was boring — and that is the finding. If a flat result is unwelcome, the answer is a different analysis, not a different axis.

Figure 2.5 — bars require a zero baseline. Lines do not, because a line encodes change through slope rather than quantity through length; a line chart may start its axis near the data, provided the axis is clearly labelled.

Axis crime 2 — the dual axis

This is the most common serious chart mistake in applied reporting. Two series with different units are plotted against two different vertical scales, and because the analyst chooses both scales, the apparent relationship between the lines is manufactured, not measured.

✖ Two y-axes
The lines track each other, so rainfall must be driving the reservoir level. Except that the two scales were chosen to make them track. Slide either axis and the story changes — including into the opposite story.
✔ Indexed to a common base
Both series set to 100 in January, so one axis serves both and the comparison is real: rainfall rose 44% while the reservoir level rose 9%. The genuine finding was invisible in the dual-axis version.

Figure 2.6 — when two measures have different units, index them to a common base, use small multiples, or draw two charts. Never two scales on one frame.

Axis crime 3 — chart junk

Every pixel that is not data is competing with the data. Gradients, 3-D extrusion, background fills, heavy borders, a gridline every five units, a value label on every bar plus a redundant percentage underneath — each one individually defensible, collectively fatal.

✖ Junk
A 3-D extrusion that makes every bar's true height ambiguous, a hatched background, seventeen gridlines, double labelling, a decorative title, and a caption saying nothing. The reader's eye has nowhere to land.
✔ Clean
Recessive gridlines, no background, flat bars, and colour used once — to mark the weekend, which is the finding. Only the two highlighted values are labelled. The title carries the point.

Figure 2.7 — the 3-D crime is the worst of these: extrusion adds a dimension carrying no information while destroying the reader's ability to judge the one that does.

And sometimes the right visual is a table

Fortino treats tables as one of the three aspects of the Charts dimension, and rightly. A table beats a chart whenever the reader needs to look up a specific value rather than perceive a pattern, when there are few numbers, or when the numbers carry different units. A well-built table is a visual: right-align numbers so digits line up, use consistent decimal places, put the comparison you care about in adjacent columns, and add one summary row.

⚠️ The one-question test for any chart

Before you ship a visual, answer this: "What is the one sentence a reader should be able to say after three seconds?" If you cannot state it, the chart has no story and no title. If you can state it but the chart does not make it obvious, the encoding is wrong. If the sentence would change when someone adjusts an axis, the chart is not honest.

🏁 30-second recap

Colour has four jobs and pastels do none of them for data — validate, don't eyeball, and never encode identity by colour alone. Bars need a zero baseline. Never two y-axes: index to a common base instead. Strip every non-data pixel. When the reader needs to look up a number, build a table.

🚦 The decision cards

⚖️ Mean or median?

Symmetric, no outliers, and you need it for later inference → mean + SD. Skewed, or outliers present, or ordinal → median + IQR. Unsure → report both; the gap is itself a finding.

🧹 Found a weird value?

Ask in order: is it an error (fix or treat as missing) · a genuine extreme from the same population (keep, flag, report median too) · from a different population (exclude and state the rule).

🚫 Missing ≠ zero

A blank means "unknown"; a zero means "none". Confusing them shifted the case-study mean by 7.7%. Mean-imputation preserves the mean but shrinks the variance. Flag and disclose instead.

📊 Which chart?

Magnitude → sorted bar. Time → line. Distribution → histogram or boxplot. Relationship → scatter. Parts of a whole → stacked bar, or a pie with ≤ 3 slices. One headline number → write the number.

🎨 Colour's four jobs

Categorical (identity, fixed order) · sequential (one hue, light→dark) · diverging (two hues, grey midpoint) · status (reserved). Anything else is decoration. One series = one colour.

👁️ Fortino's six

Story · Signs · Purpose · Perception · Method · Charts — six dimensions, three aspects each, eighteen directions of analysis.

🧾 Key terms glossary

CodebookThe reference document defining every variable's name, meaning, unit, measurement level, permitted range, category coding and missing-value convention.
Audit trailA log of every change made to the raw data and the reason for it. The raw file itself is never edited.
MCAR / MAR / MNARMissing completely at random / at random given observed variables / not at random. Only MNAR biases results in a way that no simple fix repairs.
Mean imputationReplacing a missing value with the variable's mean. Preserves the mean but artificially reduces the variance.
OutlierAn observation far from the rest. Not synonymous with error — it may be a genuine extreme or a member of a different population.
Frequency distributionA table grouping a quantitative variable into mutually exclusive, exhaustive classes with their counts.
Class boundary / midpointThe true dividing point between adjacent classes; the midpoint is the class's representative value in grouped computation.
Sturges' rulek ≈ 1 + 3.322 log₁₀ n — a starting estimate for the number of classes in a histogram.
Cross-tabulationA table of joint frequencies for two categorical variables. Percentage across the explanatory variable.
Weighted meanAn average in which each value is multiplied by a weight reflecting its group's size or importance.
Resistant statisticOne that barely changes when an extreme value is added or removed — the median and IQR are resistant; the mean, SD and range are not.
Degrees of freedomThe number of values free to vary once a parameter has been estimated. Dividing by n − 1 corrects the bias in a sample variance.
Coefficient of variationStandard deviation as a percentage of the mean. Unit-free, so it compares spread across different variables and scales.
Chebyshev's theoremFor any distribution, at least 1 − 1/k² of observations lie within k standard deviations of the mean.
Empirical ruleFor bell-shaped distributions only: roughly 68%, 95% and 99.7% within one, two and three standard deviations.
PercentileThe value below which a stated percentage of observations fall.
Interquartile rangeQ₃ − Q₁, the span of the middle 50% of the data. The resistant companion to the standard deviation.
z-score(x − x̄)/s — an observation's distance from the mean in standard-deviation units. Unit-free.
Five-number summaryMinimum, Q₁, median, Q₃, maximum — the basis of the boxplot.
SkewnessAsymmetry. Positive (right) skew pulls the mean above the median; negative skew pulls it below.
KurtosisTail weight relative to the normal distribution — how much variance comes from rare extremes. Not "peakedness".
Chart junkAny visual element that consumes attention without conveying information: 3-D extrusion, gradients, background fills, redundant labels.
Action titleA chart title stating the finding rather than the topic. "Dropout has doubled since April", not "Monthly Dropout Rate".
Dual-axis chartTwo series plotted on two different vertical scales. The apparent relationship is chosen by the designer, so the form should not be used.

Why the reported standard deviation is uninterpretable. A standard deviation carries the units of the variable it describes. Row 14 contributes a deviation of roughly 493,000 − 35,681 ≈ 457,000, and that deviation is squared before being summed — so this one mistyped cell supplies essentially the entire sum of squares. The resulting figure of 131,626 thousand m³ is not an overstatement of variability; it is a measurement of the typing error and nothing else. Note also that it is nearly four times the reported mean, giving a coefficient of variation of 369%, which for a network of similar lowland gauging stations is on its face impossible. A CV that implausible is itself a data-quality alarm, and the assistant should have stopped there rather than reporting the number.

The compounding failure. The assistant's recommendation — a recalibration programme to reduce station-to-station inconsistency — is a real proposal with real cost, and it was generated entirely by a typing error. Nothing in the corrected data supports it. This is the practical case for profiling every column before computing anything: the cost of ten minutes of range-checking is measured against the cost of a programme launched to solve a problem that does not exist.

  • Mean 543 thousand m³ reported alongside and clearly labelled as network-average streamflow, since mean × station count reconstructs the total — a legitimate and separate question.
  • Angat Confluence disclosed separately at 1,268 thousand m³ with its own commentary, not folded into a network average.
  • n = 12 with one disclosed omission (San Simon), and a note that Hagonoy is excluded from the clarity figure pending re-collection.

What the corrected figures recommend — and it is the opposite of the assistant's conclusion. Excluding the main-stem gauge, the eleven tributary stations have a coefficient of variation of 4.97%. That is an exceptionally consistent set of gauges; there is essentially no station-to-station inconsistency to fix, and the proposed recalibration programme would spend money solving a problem created by a typing error. The genuine findings are three: (1) the tributary network is tightly consistent and predictable, which is a strength worth reporting as such; (2) Angat Confluence is a materially different river — 2.6× the median streamflow on 2.2× the median sample count — and needs its own rating curve, sampling schedule and benchmarks rather than a network average that fits neither it nor its peers; and (3) the reporting process itself is the actual defect, since four field teams on four templates produced four separate data errors in a single 14-row extract. The recommendation is a shared template and codebook, not a gauge-recalibration programme.

Essay 3 · 15 points · Evaluate & Create
Critique the three slides in the visual set. For each, name the specific defect, identify which of Fortino's six dimensions it sits in, and specify the replacement chart — its form, its ordering, its axis treatment, its colour assignment and its action title. Then state the one principle that all three slides violate.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • Each defect tied to a named Fortino dimension, not just criticised.
  • A replacement specification concrete enough to hand to a designer — form, order, axis, colour, title.
  • An actual action title written out for each, not a description of one.
  • Recognition that slide 3's defect is structural, not cosmetic.
  • A unifying principle stated at the level of encoding, not style.
✅ Model answer

Slide 1 — pie chart, one slice per station.
Defect: wrong form for the job. The panel's question is a ranked magnitude comparison, and thirteen angular segments cannot be ranked by eye. There is no inherent order to the slices, the legend forces constant back-and-forth eye movement, and thirteen distinguishable hues do not exist.
Fortino dimension: Charts (right chart / selection), with a secondary failure in Method (colour doing no useful job) and Perception (angle is among the least accurately judged encodings).
Replacement: a horizontal bar chart, sorted descending, one bar per station, each labelled beside its own bar so no legend is required. A single hue, because there is one series — with a second hue used only to mark Angat Confluence, since its separateness is the finding. Value labels at the bar ends; zero baseline. Action title: "Angat Confluence alone carries 19% of network streamflow — 2.6× the typical station."

Slide 2 — sub-basin streamflow bars with the axis starting at 1,900 thousand m³.
Defect: truncated baseline. A bar encodes quantity as length from zero, so shifting the baseline means the bars now encode distance from 1,900 thousand m³. Modest sub-basin differences will appear dramatic, and the reader has no way to recover the true proportions from the picture.
Fortino dimension: Signs — the marks no longer signify what the reader will assume they signify. There is a further failure in Method, since "Figure 2: Streamflow by Sub-basin" is a topic title that delegates all interpretation to the reader.
Replacement: the same bar chart with the axis at zero. If the sub-basin differences are then too small to see, that is the finding and it should be stated in words rather than manufactured by the axis. If the fine differences genuinely matter, switch to a form that legitimately supports a tight axis — a dot plot, or a bar chart of the differences from the regional average. Action title: "All four sub-basins discharge within 8% of each other — no sub-basin needs a different sampling frequency."

Slide 3 — samples logged and clarity on two vertical axes.
Defect: structural, not cosmetic. With two independent scales the analyst chooses how closely the two lines appear to track, so the apparent relationship is manufactured rather than measured. Slide either axis and the story changes — including into the opposite story. The problem is compounded three ways here: the clarity axis runs to 9.0, a range only reachable because of the uncorrected 1–10 scale error, so the chart is drawing a data defect; station is a categorical variable, so connecting the points with lines falsely asserts continuity between one station and the next; and the title "Busier stations are clearer stations" makes a causal claim from a design that cannot support even an association.
Fortino dimension: Story — the chart tells a story the data does not contain. Also Signs (the second axis misrepresents what the marks mean) and Purpose (no framed analytical question is being answered).
Replacement: a scatterplot — samples logged on the horizontal axis, clarity on the vertical, one point per station, no connecting lines. This is the correct form for a relationship between two quantitative variables measured on the same units of analysis, and it reveals what the line chart hid: whether the association is linear, and whether Angat Confluence is driving it single-handedly. Angat Confluence labelled directly. Clarity axis on the documented 1–5 range, with Hagonoy excluded pending re-collection. Action title: "Clarity shows no clear relationship with station volume (n = 12)" — or whatever the corrected scatter actually shows. Note the count carefully: 13 unique stations remain once the duplicate row is removed, and only Hagonoy is unusable here, because San Simon and Guagua are defective on streamflow while their transaction and clarity values are both intact. That gives twelve usable points — and with only twelve, any correlation must be reported with its uncertainty and without causal language.

The principle all three violate. Every visual encoding is a promise about what the marks mean, and all three slides break that promise. The pie promises that angle conveys comparable magnitude, when it does not. The truncated bars promise that length conveys quantity, when they convey distance from an arbitrary line. The dual axis promises that vertical proximity conveys relationship, when it conveys a scaling the analyst selected. In each case the numbers are accurate and the chart still misleads — which is why chart criticism is a matter of encoding integrity rather than of taste. The three-second test settles all three: if the sentence a reader takes away would change when someone adjusts an axis or a scale, the chart is not honest.
