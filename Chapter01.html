1.1Why this chapter exists

Every field of research and professional practice is surrounded by numbers but still needs claims it can defend. The gap between data and defensible conclusions is what this course is about, and Session 1 is where that gap gets named.

Here is the situation. A public-health researcher opens a dashboard and sees that this month's average clinic waiting time is 47 minutes. That number is true. It is also, on its own, limited — because it describes only the visits captured in that dashboard. The moment the researcher says "patients typically wait about 47 minutes," something significant has happened. She has stopped reporting and started claiming. She has extended a fact about the available records into a statement about people she never observed.

That extension is called inference, and it is one of the most valuable — and most abused — moves in statistical analysis. This course exists because that move can be done well or badly, and because from the outside the two look identical. Both produce a confident sentence with a number in it.

🧠 The core idea of the whole course

A statistical claim is a promise about people you did not observe, backed by people you did. Everything you will learn — sampling, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, regression — exists to make that promise honest, and to attach a number to how much it might be wrong.

The "advanced" in Advanced Statistics

Your prerequisite course taught you to describe: means, medians, standard deviations, charts. Excellent tools. But every one of them answers a question about a data set that is already sitting in front of you. This course answers a harder question:

Given this sample, what may I responsibly claim about everyone — and how confident may I be?

Notice that the question has two halves, and the second half is the one people skip. Anyone can produce a claim. What separates a professional from a person with a spreadsheet is the ability to say how sure they are, and why — and to say it before someone spends ₱4 million acting on it.

💼 Two sentences, one dataset

Sentence A: "The 312 customers who answered our survey rated us 4.2 out of 5 on average."

Sentence B: "Our customers rate us 4.2 out of 5."

Sentence A is a description. It cannot be wrong — you can verify it by re-adding the column. Sentence B is an inference. It can absolutely be wrong, and whether it is wrong depends entirely on how those 312 people ended up in the survey. Most bad analytics in the real world is Sentence A quietly re-typed as Sentence B.

🏁 30-second recap

Description is safe and limited. Inference is powerful and risky. This course is the risk-management system for inference.

1.2Descriptive vs. inferential statistics

These are not two topics. They are two jobs, and the same number can do either job depending on the sentence you put it in.

 Descriptive statisticsInferential statistics
JobSummarise the data you actually haveDraw a conclusion about data you don't have
Scope of the claimThe observed set onlyA larger population
Can it be "wrong"?Only through arithmetic errorYes — through bad sampling, bad assumptions, or bad luck
Typical toolsMean, median, SD, frequency table, chartConfidence interval, hypothesis test, regression model

⚠️ The mistake that starts everything

Computing a descriptive statistic and then speaking as though it were an inferential one. No formula protects you here — the error happens in the sentence, not in the maths. When you read a statistical report, underline every noun that names a group. Then ask: was that group actually sampled?

When description is genuinely enough

An important nuance most students miss: if you have the entire population, inference is unnecessary. If HR has all 1,842 employee records and computes mean tenure, that mean is the parameter. There is nothing to infer and no margin of error to report. A census ends the conversation.

This matters more than it sounds. Many administrative and research datasets are censuses, not samples: all admissions in a registry, all observations from a complete monitoring period, or all employees on payroll. Reporting a "margin of error" on a census is a sign the analyst does not understand what they are doing. Conversely, treating a convenience sample as though it were a census is the more common and more consequential error.

🧪 Try it — 20 seconds

Which of these is inferential?
(a) "Our Shopee store had 12,400 visitors last week."   (b) "Roughly 3% of Filipino online shoppers abandon carts due to shipping fees."   (c) "Branch 7 sold the most units in October."

Answer: only (b) — it claims something about a population nobody fully observed. (a) and (c) are counts of records already in hand.

1.3The four building blocks

Almost every catastrophic analytics failure can be diagnosed by carefully writing down four things. Most people write down one.

🌍PopulationEveryone you want the claim to cover. Defined by you, in words, before you collect anything.
📋Sampling frameThe actual list you can draw from. Almost never identical to the population.
👥SampleThe units you actually observed and measured.
🔢Parameter / statisticThe unknown truth vs. your estimate of it.

The frame is where studies die

The sampling frame is the operational list from which your sample is actually drawn — the loyalty database, the payroll file, the followers of an account, the customers who happened to walk past the mall booth. Students consistently skip it because it feels like an administrative detail. It is not. It is the join between your ambition and your reality.

When the frame excludes part of the population, you get coverage error, and here is the brutal part: coverage error does not shrink when you collect more data. Surveying 50,000 app users instead of 5,000 tells you more about app users, and exactly nothing more about the customers who never installed the app.

💼 Case in miniature — the delivery app

A food-delivery company wants to know how satisfied all its riders are. It sends an in-app survey.

  • Population: all active riders in the country.
  • Frame: riders who opened the app during the 5-day survey window.
  • Sample: the 1,430 who tapped through and finished.
  • Who's invisible: riders who quit last month (the unhappiest ones), riders on leave, riders whose phones can't run the update.

Consequence: satisfaction will be overestimated, no matter how large the sample is. The people most likely to be dissatisfied removed themselves from the frame by leaving. This specific pattern has a name — survivorship bias — and it is common across observational data.

Parameter vs. statistic: get the notation right now

This is the notational habit that will keep you sane for the rest of the course. Greek letters describe populations. Roman letters describe samples.

QuantityParameter (population, unknown)Statistic (sample, computed)Read it as
Meanμ (mu)x̄ (x-bar)Average
Standard deviationσ (sigma)sTypical spread
Varianceσ²s²Spread, squared
Proportionπ or pp̂ (p-hat)Share / percentage

🧠 Why this isn't pedantry

A parameter is a fixed number you will never know. A statistic is a random number you happen to have computed — take a different sample and it changes. Every technique in this course is a controlled argument from the random thing to the fixed thing. If you blur them in your writing, you will blur them in your thinking, and you will end up saying things like "there's a 95% chance μ is between…" — which is wrong, and which you'll learn to fix in Session 5.

🏁 30-second recap

Population = ambition. Frame = reality. Sample = evidence. Parameter = the truth. Statistic = your guess. Write all five down before you compute anything.

1.4Variables and the roles they play

A variable is any characteristic that takes different values across units. Trivial. What is not trivial is that the same variable plays different roles in different studies, and the role determines how you must treat it.

🎚️Independent variable (IV)The presumed cause or predictor. The thing you change, choose, or use to explain. e.g. training hours
🎯Dependent variable (DV)The outcome you care about. e.g. sales per employee
🔒Control variableSomething you hold constant or statistically adjust for, so it can't muddy the IV→DV picture. e.g. branch size
👻ConfounderA lurking third variable that affects both IV and DV, faking a relationship. e.g. peak season
🎛️ModeratorChanges the strength or direction of IV→DV. Answers "for whom / when does this work?" e.g. store region
🔗MediatorThe mechanism through which IV affects DV. Answers "how does this work?" e.g. employee confidence
⚠️ Moderator vs. mediator — the classic mix-up

A moderator sits beside the arrow and bends it: training raises sales, but only in high-traffic branches. A mediator sits on the arrow and carries it: training raises confidence, and confidence raises sales. Memory hook: moderator = modifies the effect; mediator = the medium the effect travels through.

Confounders are the reason "correlation ≠ causation" exists

You've heard the slogan. Here is the machinery underneath it. Suppose aircon sales and cold-drink sales rise together across the year. Nobody thinks aircons cause thirst. A third variable — temperature — drives both. Temperature is a confounder: it is associated with the IV and it independently causes the DV, so it manufactures an association that has no causal content.

In applied data, confounders are often ordinary and powerful: seasonality, site size, baseline risk, or the fact that a new program was first assigned to the highest-performing sites. That last one — selection into treatment — is among the most common confounders in reports claiming that an initiative worked.

🧪 Try it — label the roles

A bank finds that clients who use its mobile app have 22% higher deposits. Someone proposes: "Push the app, deposits will rise."

Where's the confounder? Younger, higher-income, more financially engaged clients are both more likely to use the app and more likely to hold larger balances. Income and financial engagement are confounders. Without controlling for them — or randomising app adoption — the 22% is an association, not an effect.

Discrete vs. continuous

🔢DiscreteCountable, with gaps between possible values. Number of complaints, units sold, employees, branches. You cannot have 3.7 complaints.
📈ContinuousCan take any value in an interval, limited only by your instrument. Delivery time, revenue, weight, temperature.

The subtlety students miss: a variable can be continuous in nature but discrete as recorded. Delivery time is genuinely continuous, but if your system logs it in whole minutes, the recorded data looks discrete. The right move is to treat the variable according to its nature, while remembering that the rounding limits how precisely you can speak.

🏁 30-second recap

IV explains, DV responds, controls are held fixed, confounders lie to you, moderators bend the effect, mediators carry it. Discrete = countable; continuous = measurable.

1.5Levels of measurement — the rules of legal arithmetic

This is the most practically consequential idea in the chapter. The level of measurement of a variable determines which mathematical operations are meaningful on it. Break the rule and your output is not "slightly off" — it is meaningless, even though your calculator will happily produce a number.

Four levels, increasing in power. Mnemonic: NOIR — Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio.

LevelWhat it doesCross-disciplinary examplesLegal operationsValid centre
Nominal
labels		 Names categories. No order at all. Branch code, payment method, department, industry, SKU, gender, region = and ≠ only. Count, tabulate. Mode only
Ordinal
ranks		 Orders categories, but the gaps between them are unknown and unequal. Loyalty tier (Bronze→Platinum), job grade, class rank, satisfaction (Poor→Excellent), credit rating <, >, plus counting. No arithmetic on the codes. Median, mode
Interval
equal gaps		 Equal distances between values, but zero is arbitrary — it doesn't mean "none". Calendar year, temperature in °C, IQ score, standardised index with an arbitrary base Differences, and means built from them. Ratios are meaningless. Mean, median, mode
Ratio
true zero		 Equal gaps and a meaningful zero that means "none of it". Revenue, units sold, headcount, delivery minutes, market share, age, cost Everything: +, −, ×, ÷ Mean, median, mode, geometric mean

The zero test — how to tell interval from ratio in 5 seconds

Ask: does zero mean "there is none of this thing"?

  • Ratio: ₱0 revenue means no revenue. So ₱800,000 really is twice ₱400,000.
  • Interval: 0 °C does not mean "no temperature". So 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. And the year 2000 is not "twice" the year 1000.
⚠️ Three arithmetic crimes to never commit
  1. Averaging nominal codes. If Marketing = 1, Finance = 2, IT = 3, then "mean department = 2.1" is nonsense. The numbers are name tags, not quantities.
  2. Ratio statements on interval data. "Satisfaction doubled from 2 to 4" assumes a true zero that a 1–5 scale does not have.
  3. Ranking as if the gaps were equal. The distance from Bronze to Silver is not necessarily the distance from Gold to Platinum, so treating tiers as 1-2-3-4 and averaging them invents information nobody measured.

🔥 The Likert problem — the argument you'll have your whole career

Here is the honest situation, because your textbooks disagree and you should know why.

A single Likert item ("Strongly disagree → Strongly agree", coded 1–5) is, strictly speaking, ordinal. Nobody has demonstrated that the psychological distance from "Disagree" to "Neutral" equals the distance from "Agree" to "Strongly agree". So strictly, the mean is not defensible and the median is.

In practice, much applied and social research averages Likert data. The defensible version of that practice rests on three conditions, and you should be able to state them:

  1. You are averaging a scale, not an item. A composite of 5–7 related items behaves much more like an interval variable than any single item does.
  2. The response options are visually and verbally equally spaced, so respondents plausibly treat them as equal steps.
  3. You state the assumption out loud in your methodology, rather than hoping nobody notices.
🧠 The professional standard

Treating Likert data as interval is not automatically wrong — treating it as interval silently is. The mark of a competent analyst is the sentence: "Following common practice, the five-item satisfaction scale was treated as interval-level; this assumption is acknowledged as a limitation." That one sentence is the difference between a defensible study and an indefensible one.

Why this decides your whole analysis

Measurement level isn't a labelling exercise you do at the start and forget. It is the first filter in choosing a statistical technique — the one you'll formalise in Session 12's decision framework.

If your outcome variable is…You are heading toward…Not toward…
Nominal / categoricalChi-square tests, proportions, logistic regressiont-tests, ANOVA, linear regression
OrdinalMedians, nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis), SpearmanMeans and parametric tests — unless you defend the interval assumption
Interval / ratioMeans, t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, linear regressionChi-square on the raw values
🏁 30-second recap

NOIR. Nominal = names (mode). Ordinal = ranks (median). Interval = equal gaps, fake zero (mean, no ratios). Ratio = equal gaps, real zero (everything). Measurement level is the first gate on technique choice.

1.6The hierarchy of research questions

Research questions come in four escalating levels. Each one demands more from your design than the one before, and each one licenses a stronger claim. Much analytical overreach comes from answering a Level 3 question with a Level 1 design.

  1. Describe — "What is happening?"
    Average order value, churn rate, headcount by department. Needs: accurate data. Licenses: statements about the observed set.
  2. Compare — "Is Group A different from Group B?"
    Do Region 1 and Region 2 differ in conversion? Needs: comparable groups, random sampling. Licenses: statements about population differences. → Sessions 6–8.
  3. Associate — "Do these two things move together?"
    Does ad spend track with sales? Needs: paired measurements, adequate range. Licenses: statements about relationship, never about cause. → Sessions 9–10.
  4. Predict / explain — "Given these drivers, what will the outcome be, and which drivers matter?"
    Modelling turnover from tenure, pay and supervisor rating. Needs: a model, diagnostics, enough data. Licenses: conditional prediction and partial effects. → Session 11.
💼 Same data, four questions

A retailer has 18 months of branch-level data on staffing, foot traffic and sales.
Describe: "Mean weekly sales per branch is ₱1.4M."
Compare: "Branches in malls outsell standalone branches."
Associate: "Staffing level and sales are positively correlated (r = 0.62)."
Predict: "Holding foot traffic constant, each additional staff member is associated with ₱38K more weekly sales."
Only the last one comes close to guiding a hiring decision — and even it is an association until staffing is randomised or properly controlled.

1.7Statistical significance is not importance

This section will save you from the most expensive misunderstanding in applied statistics. You'll meet the formal machinery in Session 6; you need the intuition now, because it changes how you read every report you'll ever see.

📌 Two different questions

Statistical significance answers: "Is this difference bigger than what random sampling noise would plausibly produce?" It is a question about evidence.

Practical significance answers: "Is this difference big enough to change what we do?" It is a question about consequences — and statistics cannot answer it alone. Subject-matter judgement and explicit decision criteria are also required.

The sample-size trap

Here is the mechanism you must internalise. As your sample size grows, your ability to detect tiny differences grows with it. With enough data, almost any difference becomes statistically significant — including differences far too small to care about.

ScenarioSample sizeDifference foundStatistically significant?Worth acting on?
Website A/B test on button colour480,000+0.03% conversionYesAlmost certainly not
Pilot of a new onboarding program24+11 days retentionNoPossibly — investigate further
Supplier defect-rate comparison2,1004.1% vs 1.2%YesYes — switch supplier

Notice row 2 carefully. A non-significant result is not proof that nothing is happening. It may simply mean you didn't collect enough data to tell. "We found no significant difference" and "we found that there is no difference" are wildly different sentences, and confusing them kills good ideas prematurely.

🧠 The fix: always report an effect size

A p-value tells you whether an effect is detectable. An effect size tells you whether it is meaningful — how big the difference actually is, in units a manager understands. Reporting one without the other is professional negligence. You'll compute effect sizes formally in Session 7; for now, train the habit of asking "significant — fine, but how big?"

⚠️ Phrases that should trigger suspicion
  • "Statistically significant" with no number attached to the size of the effect.
  • "Highly significant" used to mean "large". It doesn't — it means the evidence is strong, not the effect.
  • "No significant difference, so the two are the same."
  • "The p-value was 0.049, so we're 95% sure the treatment works."

1.8Data ethics, privacy, and the misuse of statistics

Statistics is unusually easy to weaponise, because the audience usually can't check. That asymmetry is exactly why the profession carries ethical obligations — and in the Philippines, legal ones.

The legal floor: R.A. 10173

The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173) governs how personal information may be collected, processed, stored and shared. For your purposes as a student researcher, the operative principles are:

✅ConsentFreely given, specific, informed, and evidenced. Bundled or assumed consent is not consent.
🎯Legitimate purposeData is collected for a declared purpose and may not be quietly repurposed later.
⚖️ProportionalityCollect only what the research question actually requires. "Might be useful someday" is not a justification.
🔐 Anonymised vs. pseudonymised — students get this wrong constantly

Pseudonymised: you replaced names with codes, but you (or someone) keep the key. The data is still personal data and still fully regulated.

Anonymised: re-identification is genuinely impossible for anyone, because the key is destroyed and the remaining fields can't triangulate an individual. Only this is outside the regime. Beware: "Grade 12 female student from Barangay X who works part-time at Y" is not anonymous just because you deleted the name — a small enough cell size re-identifies people by itself.

The professional floor: how analysts cheat

These are the specific behaviours the course will penalise, and the ones your future employer will fire you for.

🎣p-hackingRunning many tests, or slicing the data many ways, until something crosses p < 0.05 — then reporting only that.
🔮HARKingHypothesising After the Results are Known: inspecting the data, then writing the hypothesis as if you'd predicted it.
🍒Cherry-pickingReporting the flattering subgroup, quarter, or metric and omitting the rest.
📉Chart deceptionTruncated axes, dual axes chosen to imply a link, 3-D pies, missing baselines.
🧟Survivorship biasStudying only what remains — successful firms, current customers, employees who stayed.
🧮Aggregation tricksChoosing the grouping that produces the desired story, when a different grouping reverses it.
💼 Why Simpson's paradox should scare you

A company reports that its new sales training raised close rates overall. Broken out by experience level, the trained group did worse in both the junior and senior segments — the overall gain came entirely from the fact that more seniors happened to be in the trained group. Both statements are arithmetically true. Which one you publish is an ethical choice, not a statistical one. This reversal is called Simpson's paradox, and you'll meet it again in Session 11 under the heading of confounding.

🧭 The one-line ethical test

Before you publish a finding, ask: "If the person most harmed by this claim saw my full method, would they say I was fair?" If you would need to hide a step to survive that question, the step is the problem — not the question.

🏁 30-second recap

R.A. 10173 sets the legal floor: consent, declared purpose, proportionality. Pseudonymised ≠ anonymised. The professional floor is higher: no p-hacking, no HARKing, no cherry-picking, no chart tricks. Legal is the minimum, not the goal.

🚦 The 5 questions to ask about any statistical claim

  1. Who is this about? Name the population in words.
  2. Who could actually have been picked? Name the frame, and name who it excludes.
  3. Is this a description or a claim? If a claim, was there randomness?
  4. What kind of variable is this? Does the arithmetic used match its measurement level?
  5. Significant — but how big? Would this change the decision?

🧾 Key terms glossary

PopulationThe complete set of units a claim is meant to cover, defined by the researcher before data collection.
Sampling frameThe actual list or mechanism from which sample units can be drawn. The gap between it and the population is coverage error.
SampleThe subset of units actually observed and measured.
CensusObservation of every unit in the population. Makes inference unnecessary.
ParameterA fixed numerical characteristic of a population; generally unknown. Greek letters.
StatisticA numerical characteristic computed from a sample; varies from sample to sample. Roman letters.
Coverage errorBias arising because the frame omits part of the population. Not reduced by larger samples.
Survivorship biasDistortion from analysing only units that remained, ignoring those that dropped out or failed.
Level of measurementThe classification (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) that determines which operations are meaningful.
ConfounderA third variable associated with the predictor and independently causing the outcome, manufacturing a spurious association.
ModeratorA variable that changes the strength or direction of a relationship. Answers "for whom / under what conditions".
MediatorA variable that transmits the effect of the predictor to the outcome. Answers "through what mechanism".
Statistical significanceA judgement that an observed result is unlikely under the assumption of no real effect. About evidence, not magnitude.
Practical significanceA judgement that an effect is large enough to matter for decisions. Requires domain knowledge, not statistics.
Effect sizeA standardised measure of the magnitude of a difference or relationship, independent of sample size.
p-hackingRepeatedly testing or slicing data until a significant result appears, then reporting only that result.
HARKingPresenting a hypothesis formed after seeing the results as though it had been predicted in advance.
Simpson's paradoxA reversal in which an association present in aggregated data disappears or flips within every subgroup.
PseudonymisationReplacing identifiers with codes while retaining a re-identification key. Data remains personal data under R.A. 10173.
AnonymisationIrreversible removal of identifiability, including through combinations of fields. Only this exits the privacy regime.

✍️Applied Case Analysis

Everything in this chapter converges here. Read the case, then answer three essay questions. Model answers and scoring rubrics are provided — but write yours before you open them, or the exercise is worthless.

☕ Case: Barako Blend Coffee Co. — "Did the app work?"

Background. Barako Blend operates 48 branches — 42 across Metro Manila and 6 in Cebu. In June it launched Kapehan Rewards, a mobile loyalty app. By December the app had 21,500 registered users. Internal POS data estimates the chain serves roughly 140,000 unique customers per month across all branches. Three underperforming branches were closed in September.

The study. From 1–5 December, an in-app survey was pushed to the 8,400 users who opened the app during that window. 1,120 completed it (a 13.3% response rate). The survey contained five items:

ItemQuestionResponse format
Q1Overall, how satisfied are you with Barako Blend?5-point scale, Very dissatisfied → Very satisfied, coded 1–5
Q2How many times did you visit last month?Open numeric (whole visits)
Q3Which branch do you visit most often?Dropdown list of 48 branches
Q4Approximately how much do you spend on coffee per month?Open numeric, in pesos
Q5How would you rate our baristas?5-point scale, Poor → Excellent, coded 1–5

The result. Mean Q1 = 4.31. This was compared against last year's pre-app benchmark of 4.19, which came from a mall-intercept survey of 600 customers conducted the previous March by a field agency. An independent-samples t-test on the two means returned p = 0.03.

The memo. The Marketing Head circulated this to the Executive Committee:

"Customer satisfaction among Barako Blend customers has significantly increased since the Kapehan Rewards launch (p = 0.03). The app produced a 0.12-point improvement — a 2.9% lift — and satisfaction is now 4.31 out of 5, meaning our customers are nearly twice as satisfied as the neutral midpoint. Recommend doubling the app marketing budget to ₱12M for the coming fiscal year."

One more thing. To enrich the analysis, the analytics team joined each survey response to the respondent's loyalty ID and full 6-month purchase history. The survey's only introductory text was: "Help us serve you better! 2-minute survey. 🙌"

Essay 1 · 15 points · Analyse
Identify the population, the sampling frame, and the sample in the Barako Blend study. Explain precisely which groups of customers had zero probability of appearing in the results, and state the narrowest population about which the company may honestly make a claim. Explain why increasing the survey push from 8,400 to 20,000 users would not solve the core problem.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • All three blocks named separately and specifically, not blurred together.
  • At least three distinct excluded groups, each with a reason.
  • A directional argument: does the exclusion bias satisfaction up or down, and why?
  • An explicitly narrowed population statement written as a sentence the company could publish.
  • The size-vs-bias principle stated as a principle, not just asserted.
✅ Model answer

Population. The population implied by the Marketing Head's memo is all Barako Blend customers — approximately 140,000 unique customers per month across 48 branches. That is the group the claim is about.

Sampling frame. The operational frame is far narrower: the 8,400 registered app users who opened the app between 1 and 5 December. Note that this is narrower even than the app's own user base of 21,500, because it additionally requires having opened the app in one specific five-day window.

Sample. The 1,120 users who completed the survey — 13.3% of those pushed, and roughly 0.8% of the monthly customer population.

Who had zero probability of selection.

  • The ~118,500 monthly customers who never registered for the app. This is the largest exclusion by far. Non-adopters plausibly differ systematically — older customers, walk-ins, those without smartphones or data, and crucially those less engaged with the brand.
  • Registered users who did not open the app that week (21,500 − 8,400 = 13,100). Lapsed or disengaged users are concentrated here, and disengagement correlates with dissatisfaction.
  • Customers of the three branches closed in September. If those branches were closed for underperformance, their customers were plausibly the least satisfied — and they have been structurally deleted from the data.
  • Former customers who churned before December for any reason. The unhappiest people leave, and leaving removes them from the frame.

Direction of the bias. Every one of these exclusions removes people who are less engaged or more likely to be dissatisfied. The mean of 4.31 is therefore biased upward. This is a textbook combination of coverage error and survivorship bias, compounded by non-response bias among the 86.7% who ignored the survey.

The narrowest honest claim. "Among Barako Blend loyalty-app users who opened the app during the first week of December and chose to respond, mean self-reported satisfaction was 4.31 on a five-point scale." Every clause in that sentence is doing necessary work, and none may be dropped.

Why a bigger push doesn't help. Sample size governs sampling error — random noise, which shrinks as n grows. It has no effect whatsoever on coverage error, which is systematic and comes from who is in the frame at all. Pushing to 20,000 users would produce a more precise estimate of app-user satisfaction while leaving the estimate of customer satisfaction exactly as wrong — only now with a narrower confidence interval around the wrong number. Precision without validity is more dangerous than imprecision, because it looks authoritative. The only genuine fixes are frame-level: intercept sampling at branches, receipt-based QR invitations to all customers, or an explicit stratified design that reaches non-app users.

Essay 2 · 15 points · Evaluate
Classify each of the five survey items by level of measurement. Then evaluate two specific claims in the Marketing Head's memo: (a) that satisfaction is "nearly twice as satisfied as the neutral midpoint," and (b) that the 4.31 figure is directly comparable to the 4.19 benchmark. For each, state whether the claim is defensible, and if not, what would have to change for it to become so.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • All five items classified, with justification — including the Q2/Q4 subtlety.
  • The zero test applied explicitly to the "twice as satisfied" claim.
  • Recognition that a single Likert item is strictly ordinal, plus the conditions under which averaging is defensible.
  • At least three distinct threats to comparability between the two surveys.
  • Concrete, actionable remedies — not just criticism.
✅ Model answer

Classification of the five items.

ItemLevelJustification
Q1Ordinal (commonly treated as interval)Ordered categories, but the psychological gap between "Dissatisfied" and "Neutral" is not demonstrably equal to that between "Satisfied" and "Very satisfied". Strictly the median is the defensible centre.
Q2Ratio (discrete)Zero visits genuinely means no visits, and 8 visits is genuinely twice 4. Discrete because visits are countable whole numbers.
Q3NominalBranch names are labels with no inherent order. Only counts, proportions and the mode are meaningful. The dropdown's internal index numbers are name tags, not quantities.
Q4Ratio (continuous in nature)₱0 means no spending; ₱2,000 is twice ₱1,000. Note it is self-reported, which is a data-quality problem, not a measurement-level problem.
Q5Ordinal (commonly treated as interval)Same reasoning as Q1.

(a) "Nearly twice as satisfied as the neutral midpoint" — indefensible.

This claim requires a ratio scale, and the satisfaction item does not have one. Apply the zero test: on a 1–5 scale, the value 1 does not represent an absence of satisfaction — it represents the label "Very dissatisfied", which is a substantive state, not zero. There is no true zero anywhere on the scale, so the ratio 4.31 ÷ 3 = 1.44 is arithmetically computable but semantically empty. Worse, the coding is arbitrary: had the same scale been coded −2 to +2, the identical responses would yield a mean of 0.31 against a midpoint of 0, and the "ratio" would be infinite. A conclusion that changes when you relabel the categories is not a finding about customers.

What would make it defensible: nothing about this scale. The claim must be restated in the only language the data supports — "the mean response falls between 'Satisfied' and 'Very satisfied'" or, better, a distributional statement: "68% of respondents selected 4 or 5." Percentages of the top box are both more honest and more useful to management than a mean on ordinal data.

(b) Comparability of 4.31 with 4.19 — not defensible as it stands. At least four distinct threats:

  1. Different modes. An in-app self-administered survey and a face-to-face mall intercept produce different answers from the same person. Interviewer presence reliably inflates positive responses through social-desirability bias; conversely, an app survey reaches an already-committed audience. The two modes are not interchangeable instruments.
  2. Different populations. The benchmark sampled mall-intercept customers — including non-app users. The new survey sampled only engaged app users. This is not the same population measured twice; it is two different populations measured once each.
  3. Different seasons. March versus December. December is peak Christmas trading with seasonal drinks, holiday promotions and festive store atmosphere. Season is a textbook confounder: it plausibly raises satisfaction independently of the app, and it is perfectly correlated with the "treatment".
  4. Different instruments and anchors. The case does not establish that the two surveys used identical wording, identical anchor labels, or identical question order. Any of these changes shifts a mean by more than 0.12.

What would make it defensible: measure the same population, with the same instrument, in the same mode, at the same point in the annual cycle — ideally a pre-registered before-and-after design with a comparison group of non-adopters, or at minimum a December-to-December comparison. Absent that, the honest statement is that the two figures cannot be differenced, and the 0.12 "improvement" is not a measured quantity at all.

Essay 3 · 20 points · Evaluate & Create
You are the analyst asked to review the memo before it reaches the Executive Committee. Write a response of roughly 400–500 words that (i) explains what p = 0.03 does and does not establish, (ii) evaluates whether a 0.12-point difference is practically significant and what evidence would settle that, (iii) assesses the causal claim that "the app produced" the improvement, and (iv) identifies the ethical and legal problems in how the data was collected and joined, with reference to R.A. 10173. Conclude with a specific recommendation on the ₱12M budget request.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • A correct definition of what a p-value is conditional on — not "the probability the app worked".
  • Explicit separation of statistical from practical significance, with a stated decision threshold.
  • Named confounders and the design that would be needed for a causal claim.
  • The consent problem identified specifically, not vaguely — purpose limitation and proportionality.
  • A recommendation that is actionable and does not simply say "reject".
✅ Model answer

(i) What p = 0.03 establishes. It says that if the two population means were truly equal, a difference at least as large as the one observed would occur in about 3% of repeated samples. It is a statement about data given an assumption, not about the probability that the assumption is false. It is emphatically not a 97% chance that the app worked, and it says nothing about the size of any difference. Critically, a p-value is only meaningful when the data came from a random sample of the population of interest — which, as established in Essay 1, it did not. Applied to a self-selected sample from a coverage-deficient frame, the p-value is answering a question nobody asked.

(ii) Practical significance. A 0.12-point shift on a five-point scale is 2.4% of the scale range. With n = 1,120, statistical significance is unsurprising — large samples make small differences detectable, which is exactly the trap the memo has fallen into. The relevant question is not whether the difference is detectable but whether it is actionable: does a 0.12-point movement in a self-reported score predict any change in revisit rate, basket size or churn? That is an empirical question the company can answer from its own POS data, and until it does, there is no basis for valuing the change at ₱12M. I would require an effect size and, ideally, a demonstrated link between satisfaction movements of this magnitude and a revenue outcome before treating the result as material.

(iii) The causal claim. "The app produced" a change cannot be supported by this design. There is no control group, no randomisation, and no pre-post measurement on the same individuals. At least three confounders are live: seasonality (December vs March), self-selection (people who choose to adopt a loyalty app are already more favourable toward the brand — the app did not make them loyal, their loyalty made them install the app), and survivorship (three underperforming branches were closed, removing their dissatisfied customers from the data entirely). Any of these alone could generate the entire 0.12. To support a causal claim the company would need, at minimum, a comparison of adopters against matched non-adopters over the same period, and preferably a staged rollout that creates a genuine control group.

(iv) Ethical and legal problems. The survey invitation stated only "Help us serve you better!" Respondents were not told that their answers would be linked to their loyalty ID and six months of purchase history. Under R.A. 10173 this fails on at least two counts. Consent must be informed and specific; consent to a two-minute satisfaction survey is not consent to behavioural profiling. Purpose limitation means personal data may not be processed for purposes beyond those declared at collection, and the linkage is precisely such a repurposing. Proportionality is also questionable: a satisfaction study does not require six months of transaction-level history. Separately, describing responses as "anonymous" would be false — retaining the loyalty ID makes the dataset pseudonymised, not anonymised, and it therefore remains fully regulated personal data. Beyond the law, the memo's selective framing — a favourable ratio claim, a causal verb, no limitations section — is a reporting-integrity problem in its own right.

Recommendation. Do not approve the ₱12M on this evidence, and do not reject the app either — the study is uninformative in both directions, which is a different verdict from "the app failed". I recommend: (1) hold the budget at current level for one quarter; (2) commission a branch-intercept survey covering non-app users, run in the same month as the follow-up, to establish an unbiased baseline; (3) run a matched adopter/non-adopter comparison on POS data, where the outcome is observed behaviour rather than self-report; (4) suspend the survey-to-purchase-history linkage immediately, consult the Data Protection Officer, and re-collect with an explicit consent statement; and (5) re-issue the memo with the claim narrowed to app users, the causal verb removed, and a limitations section attached.
